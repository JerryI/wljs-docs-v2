## Dielectric plate

We shall start with the simplest case, where there are no losses, and our material parameters $\epsilon$ and $\mu$ are frequency independent (no Drude, no Lorentz response). In this scenario, we do not need to write additional equations for polarization and currents:

$$
\begin{align*}
\mu \frac{\partial H_{y}}{\partial t} &= \frac{\partial E_{z}}{\partial x} \\
\epsilon \frac{\partial E_{z}}{\partial t} &= \frac{\partial H_{y}}{\partial x} 
\end{align*}
$$

The first equation provides the temporal derivative of the magnetic field in terms of the spatial derivative of the electric field. Conversely, the second equation provides the temporal derivative of the electric field in terms of the spatial derivative of the magnetic field. 

We then discretize them:

$$
\begin{align*}
H_{y}[m] = H_{y}[m] + \frac{S}{\epsilon_r Z} \Big( E_{z}[m+1] - E_{z}[m] \Big) \\
E_{z}[m] = E_{z}[m] + \frac{S Z}{\mu_r} \Big( H_{y}[m] - H_{y}[m-1] \Big)
\end{align*}
$$

where $S=1.0$ is a time-space number, and $Z=\sqrt{\mu_0/\epsilon_0}$ is the vacuum impedance.

## Dielectric with Frequency-Dependent Permittivity

Let's explore further by modeling light propagation through a medium with frequency-dependent dielectric/magnetic response, such as a glass-like material with absorbing dye center impurities. Since we are working in the time domain, we cannot simply define $\varepsilon_r(\omega)$. Instead, we decompose the relative permittivity into two coupled parts:

$$
\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0 \epsilon_r \mathbf{E} = \epsilon_0 (\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P})
$$

Here, we write a separate equation and discretize it for $\mathbf{P}$. A good approximation for a dielectric is a Lorentz oscillator:

$$
\frac{\partial^2 \mathbf{P}}{\partial t^2} + \gamma \frac{\partial \mathbf{P}}{\partial t} + \omega_0^2 \mathbf{P} = \Omega \mathbf{E}
$$

In this equation, $\Omega$ is a coupling constant, $\gamma$ represents losses (proportional to optical conductivity), and $\omega_0$ is the eigenfrequency. By introducing a polarization current $\mathbf{J}$, we can reduce the second-order derivative equation to a first-order one, making it suitable for the FDTD method.

Here is a single module for modelling such scenario:
